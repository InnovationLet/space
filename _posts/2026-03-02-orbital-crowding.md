On February 10, 2009, two satellites collided over Siberia at roughly 26,000 miles per hour. Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251 created thousands of pieces of debris, a stark reminder of how quickly space can get messy. Over 15 years later, that risk is much higher.

## The numbers keep climbing

Since 2019, the number of active satellites in low Earth orbit has skyrocketed, driven mostly by large constellations. Companies are launching thousands of satellites to provide broadband coverage. That growth means more objects sharing similar altitudes, especially between 400 and 600 kilometers. More objects mean more close approaches. Even with automated tracking, the sheer volume makes it harder to predict and avoid every potential encounter.

## Collision risk compounds

Debris is self-perpetuating. One collision creates fragments that can trigger more collisions, a scenario known as the Kessler syndrome. Small pieces, some too tiny to track reliably, can still destroy satellites. Conjunction warnings are becoming more frequent for operators. For some large constellations, satellites get hundreds of alerts per year. Not every alert leads to a maneuver, and maneuvers burn fuel, shorten lifespans, and disrupt schedules.

## The regulatory gap

International space law was written when launches were rare. The Outer Space Treaty gives responsibility to launching states, but it wasn't designed for thousands of satellites from private operators. Licensing processes vary by country, and there's no single global authority enforcing traffic rules in low Earth orbit. Some regions require deorbit plans, but enforcement is uneven. Operators don't always share precise maneuver plans, making coordination harder. With more countries and companies entering the field, consensus is slow.

## Practical fixes are underway

Operators are testing automated collision avoidance, better tracking, and end-of-life disposal. Some satellites now deorbit within five years of mission end, not the decades allowed in the past. But voluntary measures aren't enough without broader coordination. The key takeaway is this: without timely updates to space traffic coordination, we'll keep adding objects faster than we can safely manage them. That puts both current missions and future access to orbit at risk.
