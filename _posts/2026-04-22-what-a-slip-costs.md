When NASA's Europa Clipper missed its original launch window in 2022, it didn't just shift dates. It triggered a chain of costs that rippled through budgets, contracts, and science teams. A launch slip can be far more expensive than people realize.

## Direct costs add up

Every extra month on the pad or in storage costs money. You still pay for mission operations staff, facility rentals, security, and sometimes storage for spacecraft and payloads. Contracts often have penalties or require renegotiation. Launch providers may charge for stand-downs or rescheduling. For flagship missions, those overruns can run into tens of millions of dollars. That money has to come from somewhere, often by cutting or delaying other work.

## Science loses time and opportunity

Many missions target specific launch windows for a reason. Planetary launches have narrow windows tied to orbital alignments. Miss one, and you might wait months or years for the next opportunity. That delays data return. For time-sensitive instruments or missions studying seasonal changes, a slip can mean losing an entire observing season. Graduate students and postdocs may see their research windows shrink. Collaborators may have to adjust grants or shift priorities entirely.

## The human toll matters

Launch slips take a psychological toll on teams. Engineers, technicians, and scientists work for years toward a launch date. When it's pushed back repeatedly, morale drops. Some people leave for other projects. Knowledge leaves with them. Families also feel the strain of shifting travel plans, especially for remote launches. It's easy to focus on budgets, but the people keeping these missions running pay a real price.

## Plan for flexibility, but don't waste it

There's no way to eliminate slips entirely. Weather, hardware issues, and range conflicts happen. The best missions build in schedule margin where possible and have contingency plans. But margin costs money too. The practical takeaway: when evaluating a mission's cost, don't just look at the baseline launch price. Account for the cost of delay. If a slip would kill your science window or break your budget, you need more margin than you think.
