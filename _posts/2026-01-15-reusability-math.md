SpaceX's Falcon 9 booster B1058 flew 19 times before retirement, a number that once seemed impossible for orbital rockets. But each extra flight doesn't automatically translate to savings for the rest of the industry. The economics of reusable rockets are more complicated than the marketing suggests.

## Sticker price vs marginal cost

When you build a new Falcon 9 first stage, it costs roughly $60 million. If you could fly it 10 times with no work between flights, the marginal cost per launch would drop to $6 million. That's the argument for reusability. But you can't just refuel and fly again. Each returned booster needs inspections, part replacements, and refurbishment. SpaceX hasn't published exact numbers, but industry estimates put refurbishment costs between $1 million and $5 million per flight depending on how hard the booster was stressed. Even at the low end, you're still paying significant costs on top of fuel and operations.

## Launch cadence changes everything

Reusability only pays off if you're flying often enough to amortize development and recovery costs. SpaceX can launch Falcon 9 multiple times per month because it has a steady stream of Starlink missions and commercial contracts. A smaller launcher with just a few flights per year won't see the same benefit. If you only fly twice annually, saving a few million per launch doesn't make up for the added complexity and recovery infrastructure. The math flips depending on how frequently you launch.

## Risk and insurance matter too

Every landing is a chance to lose a booster. Recovery systems add mass and failure points. Insurance premiums reflect that. For expendable rockets, you pay once and lose it. For reusable ones, you might save on hardware but pay more to insure a more complex system, especially early on. Those costs get baked into pricing, even if they're less visible than the headline "reuse" number.

## The real question isn't reuse or not

It's whether your mission set supports high cadence. If you're launching constellations, reusability makes sense. If you're doing one-off scientific missions to specific windows, the calculus shifts. Don't assume reuse always saves money. Look at how often you'll fly, what your refurbishment bill will be, and whether landing constraints limit your payload. That's the math that actually matters.
